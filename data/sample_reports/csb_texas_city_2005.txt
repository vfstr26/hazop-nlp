INCIDENT REPORT — BP Texas City Refinery Explosion and Fire
Date: March 23, 2005
Location: Texas City, Texas, USA
Facility: BP Products North America Inc. — Texas City Refinery
Source: U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board (CSB) Report No. 2005-04-I-TX

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

On March 23, 2005, a catastrophic explosion and fire occurred at the BP Texas City
refinery. Fifteen workers were killed and 180 others were injured. The incident was
the deadliest industrial accident in the United States in over a decade.

The explosion occurred during the startup of the raffinate splitter tower at the
isomerisation unit. A hydrocarbon liquid and vapour cloud ignited, causing a massive
explosion and fire. The raffinate splitter tower had been overfilled with liquid
hydrocarbon (raffinate), causing the liquid level to rise dangerously.

PROCESS DESCRIPTION

The raffinate splitter is a distillation column operating at approximately 100 psig
and temperatures of 250°F at the bottom. The feed is a C5-C6 hydrocarbon raffinate
stream. The tower separates lighter pentane fractions overhead from heavier hexane
bottoms product.

During the startup, operators fed raffinate into the tower over three hours without
establishing a flow out of the tower bottom. The level instrument in the tower
malfunctioned and gave a false reading, showing the level was normal when in fact
the tower was overfilling.

ROOT CAUSES

1. Organisational and safety culture deficiencies at BP Texas City plant
2. Level transmitter malfunction — the level indicator in the raffinate splitter tower
   failed and showed a falsely low level reading throughout the startup procedure.
3. Operators were not informed of multiple level transmitter failures prior to startup.
4. The blowdown drum and stack were undersized and not adequate to handle the
   large volume of hydrocarbon vapour released during the relief event.
5. Pressure relief valves opened as designed, discharging flammable hydrocarbons
   to the blowdown drum. The blowdown drum overflowed, and liquid hydrocarbons
   cascaded down the stack and spread across the ground.
6. Trailers housing contract workers were placed too close to the process unit,
   contrary to the site siting standard.

IMMEDIATE CAUSES

- Overfilling of the raffinate splitter tower with liquid hydrocarbon
- Loss of level control due to failed instrument
- Pressure relief valve opening discharging C5/C6 hydrocarbons to inadequate
  blowdown drum
- Liquid overflow from blowdown drum forming pool of flammable liquid
- Ignition of flammable vapour cloud by idling pickup truck engine

CONSEQUENCES

- 15 fatalities, all of whom were located in or near contractor trailers
- 180 injuries
- Destruction of the isom unit and surrounding structures
- Major fire burning for several days
- Evacuation of the surrounding community
- Environmental contamination from spilled hydrocarbons and firefighting water

CHEMICALS INVOLVED

- C5/C6 hydrocarbon raffinate (highly flammable, autoignition temperature ~260°C)
- Gasoline components
- Hydrogen (present in adjacent units)
- Pentane (explosive limits 1.5% to 7.8% in air)
- Hexane (explosive limits 1.1% to 7.5% in air)

EQUIPMENT FAILURES

- Raffinate splitter distillation column (level instrument failure)
- Blowdown drum — undersized for actual hydrocarbon inventory
- Blowdown stack — open-top design, not routed to flare system
- Pressure relief valves (opened as designed — not a failure, but inadequate downstream)
- Level transmitter (float-type — failed to read actual level)

SAFEGUARD FAILURES

- Level transmitter reading falsely low — operators had no true indication of tower level
- No independent high-level alarm that would have warned operators
- No interlock to stop raffinate feed on high-high level
- Blowdown drum not connected to the flare system
- Emergency response plan did not account for trailer siting near process units

RECOMMENDED SAFEGUARDS

1. Install independent redundant level measurement (1oo2 or 2oo3 voting) on
   distillation towers and all critical vessels during startup.
2. Connect all blowdown systems and pressure relief valve discharges to a closed
   flare system — eliminate all open-discharge blowdown stacks.
3. Implement a startup checklist with mandatory independent verification of all
   level instrument functionality before introducing feed.
4. Relocate non-essential personnel trailers at least 150 metres from process units
   per API RP 752 siting guidelines.
5. Establish a high-high level interlock (SIS) that automatically stops feed and
   initiates controlled shutdown on excessive level.
6. Conduct pre-startup safety reviews (PSSR) for all major equipment restarts
   following maintenance.
7. Implement a process safety management culture improvement programme addressing
   organisational failures identified in the investigation.

LESSONS LEARNED

This incident demonstrates the critical importance of:
- Reliable and redundant level instrumentation on distillation columns
- Adequate blowdown and pressure relief system design
- Proper siting of occupied buildings relative to process units
- A strong process safety culture starting at the management level
- Effective pre-startup safety reviews

The CSB found that BP Texas City had a history of similar near-miss incidents
involving the raffinate splitter and other units that were not adequately investigated
or addressed. A strong near-miss reporting culture could have prevented this tragedy.
