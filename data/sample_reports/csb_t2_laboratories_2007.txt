INCIDENT REPORT — T2 Laboratories Runaway Reaction Explosion
Date: December 19, 2007
Location: Jacksonville, Florida, USA
Facility: T2 Laboratories Inc.
Source: U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board (CSB) Report No. 2008-3-I-FL

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

On December 19, 2007, a runaway chemical reaction in a batch reactor at T2 Laboratories
caused a massive explosion and fire. Four employees were killed, 32 people outside the
plant were injured, and the facility was completely destroyed. Debris was found up to
one mile from the explosion site.

T2 Laboratories produced methylcyclopentadienyl manganese tricarbonyl (MCMT), an
organomanganese compound used as a fuel additive to boost octane in gasoline.

PROCESS DESCRIPTION

MCMT was produced in a 2,450-gallon (9,275-litre) jacketed batch reactor vessel.
The production process involved an exothermic reaction between sodium, a reactive
metal, and a hydrocarbon solvent (methylcyclopentadiene dimmer) in the presence of
manganese chloride under high pressure and elevated temperature.

The reaction was cooled by cooling water circulating through the reactor jacket.
The batch pressure was approximately 400 psig and temperature around 200°C during
normal operation.

IMMEDIATE CAUSE

On the day of the incident, cooling water to the reactor was lost during the batch
reaction. The reactor temperature rose rapidly as the exothermic reaction continued
without cooling.

The temperature increase accelerated the reaction rate exponentially (Arrhenius
kinetics), leading to thermal runaway. The reactor pressure exceeded its design
limit and the vessel burst catastrophically.

ROOT CAUSES

1. T2 Laboratories had no formal process hazard analysis (PHA) or HAZOP study
   conducted for the MCMT production process.
2. No engineering evaluation had been conducted of the consequences of loss of
   cooling — the most critical safeguard in the process.
3. The company was unaware that the MCMT production reaction could generate
   sufficient heat, if cooling was lost, to cause runaway and vessel failure.
4. The cooling system had no backup — there was a single cooling water supply
   with no redundancy, no emergency backup cooling system, and no high-temperature
   interlock to stop the reaction.
5. The reactor design pressure was insufficient for the pressure generated by a
   runaway reaction scenario.
6. The pressure relief system was not designed for the runaway reaction case —
   it was sized only for normal process upsets, not for runaway decomposition.

CONTRIBUTING FACTORS

- Lack of reactive hazard awareness by the owners and operators
- No safety management system or formal process safety procedures
- No emergency response plan for a runaway reaction scenario
- Employees had not been trained on runaway reaction hazards
- The reactor had no emergency dump or quench system

CHEMICALS INVOLVED

- Sodium metal (highly reactive, ignites on contact with water)
- Methylcyclopentadiene dimer (flammable hydrocarbon solvent, flash point < 23°C)
- Manganese chloride
- MCMT product (methylcyclopentadienyl manganese tricarbonyl)
- Hydrogen gas (generated as a by-product of reaction with sodium)

CONSEQUENCES

- 4 fatalities (all T2 employees)
- 32 injuries to people outside the facility
- Total destruction of the T2 Laboratories facility
- Blast wave caused structural damage to buildings within a quarter mile
- Shrapnel and debris landed up to one mile away
- Community evacuation

EQUIPMENT FAILURES

- Batch reactor — catastrophic failure due to overpressure
- Cooling water system — loss of cooling water flow (root cause unknown, possibly
  blockage or pump failure)
- Pressure relief system — undersized for runaway case
- No emergency cooling backup available

PROCESS PARAMETER DEVIATIONS

- Temperature: MORE — reactor temperature rose uncontrolled following loss of cooling
- Pressure: MORE — vessel pressure exceeded design pressure due to runaway decomposition
- Cooling flow: NO/NONE — complete loss of cooling water flow to reactor jacket
- Reaction rate: MORE — exponential increase due to temperature rise (Arrhenius effect)

SAFEGUARD DEFICIENCIES

- No high-temperature alarm or interlock on reactor
- No high-pressure interlock
- No backup cooling system or emergency quench capability
- No redundancy in cooling water supply
- Pressure relief system undersized for runaway scenario
- No DIERS (Design Institute for Emergency Relief Systems) analysis performed
- No reactive chemical hazard evaluation conducted

RECOMMENDED SAFEGUARDS

1. Conduct a formal process hazard analysis (HAZOP or What-If) before commencing
   production of any reactive chemical process.
2. Perform adiabatic calorimetry (ARC or VSP testing) to characterise the heat
   of reaction and any decomposition reactions for all exothermic batch processes.
3. Size emergency relief systems for the worst-case runaway reaction scenario
   using DIERS methodology (two-phase vapour-liquid relief flow).
4. Install independent redundant temperature measurement with a high-temperature
   alarm and automatic shutdown interlock (SIS) that terminates heating and
   feeds on high temperature.
5. Provide a backup emergency cooling system (independent water supply, dump tank,
   or refrigerated cooling) for all exothermic batch reactors.
6. Install an emergency dump or quench system to rapidly terminate runaway reactions.
7. Ensure pressure relief system is routed to a safe location (closed flare or
   containment vessel) rather than discharging to atmosphere.
8. Implement a reactive chemical management programme per CCPS guidelines.

REGULATORY FINDINGS

The CSB found that OSHA's Process Safety Management (PSM) standard at 29 CFR 1910.119
did not apply to T2 Laboratories because MCMT was not on the PSM covered chemical list,
even though the process used highly reactive sodium metal. This represented a gap in
the PSM regulation for reactive chemical hazards.

The CSB recommended that OSHA expand the PSM standard to cover reactive chemical
hazards not currently on the covered chemical list.

LESSONS LEARNED

1. Reactive chemical hazards require thorough evaluation even for small-scale
   batch operations — scale of production does not eliminate the hazard.
2. Loss of cooling is the most critical deviation for any exothermic reaction process
   and must be the primary focus of process hazard analysis.
3. Emergency relief systems must be sized for the worst-case runaway scenario,
   not just for normal upset conditions.
4. Process safety management applies to all industries handling hazardous chemicals,
   regardless of company size or whether regulations technically apply.
